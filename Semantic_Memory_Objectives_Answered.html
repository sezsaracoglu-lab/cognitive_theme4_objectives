Part A · Murphy — NOBA “Categories and Concepts”

A. Categories and concepts

Objective A1

How is semantic knowledge organised in memory?

Murphy’s answer: in categories that are fuzzy, graded, nested in hierarchies, and tied into our knowledge of the world.

🧭 What the question asks: according to Murphy, what does the “filing system” of knowledge look like — what are its structural properties?

✍️ Answer

According to Murphy, semantic knowledge is organised into categories, represented mentally as concepts, which allow us to generalise what we know to new objects. First, categories are not well defined: for most categories no set of necessary and jointly sufficient features exists, and membership forms a continuum — Hampton (1979) found items that were just in or just out (sinks, sponges), and McCloskey and Glucksberg (1978) found people changed their own judgments of borderline items up to 22% of the time. Second, categories have a graded internal structure: some members are more typical than others (Rosch, 1973), because they share more features with other members and fewer with other categories (family resemblance; Rosch & Mervis, 1975). Third, categories are organised in hierarchies (superordinate, basic, subordinate) with a preferred basic level that is named, learned and identified most readily (Rosch et al., 1976: 1,595 basic vs 14 subordinate vs 1 superordinate names). The basic level is the most differentiated level, but it shifts with expertise and culture. Finally, concepts are embedded in world knowledge: coherent concepts are learned faster (Murphy & Allopenna, 1994), and people often believe natural kinds have hidden essences (Keil, 1989).

🖼️ The answer in one picture — and the key points

  • 🌫️Fuzzy: no definitions; membership is a continuum and even individuals are inconsistent (Hampton, 1979; McCloskey & Glucksberg, 1978).
  • 🎯Graded: typicality — members differ in goodness; explained by family resemblance (Rosch & Mervis, 1975).
  • 🪜Hierarchical: nested levels with a preferred basic level (1,595 vs 14 vs 1 names; Rosch et al., 1976), shifted by expertise and culture.
  • 🧩Knowledge-based: concepts fit our world knowledge (donker vs blegdav) and beliefs about essences.
  • 🔭Function: concepts let us extend knowledge from a few objects to an unlimited number of new ones.
Semantic knowledge is organised into categories that are fuzzy, graded by typicality, nested in hierarchies with a preferred basic level, and embedded in our broader knowledge of the world.
🪝 Memory hook: F-G-H-K — Fuzzy, Graded, Hierarchical, Knowledge-based.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: “We sort knowledge into categories, but they have blurry edges, some members are better examples than others, they’re stacked in levels where we prefer the middle one (‘chair’), and they only make sense against everything else we know about the world.”
⚠️ Where marks are lost
  • Listing only “categories and hierarchies” — include fuzziness, typicality and knowledge.
  • Giving no study for a claim; each principle has a named study with a result.
Objective A2

How are individual categories represented in memory?

Four answers — and the current view that we probably use several of them.

🧭 What the question asks: when you know what a “bird” is, what exactly is stored in your head — a rule, an average, a set of examples, or a theory?

✍️ Answer

Four views have been proposed. The classical view represents a category as a definition: a set of necessary and jointly sufficient features, so membership is all-or-none. It fails for most natural categories. The prototype view (Smith & Medin, 1981) holds that we store a summary representation — a list of features weighted by their frequency in the category — and classify a new item by how well it matches this summary; Posner, Goldsmith and Welton (1967) showed that people abstract such a prototype, falsely “recognising” a dot pattern they had never seen. The exemplar view (Medin & Schaffer, 1978) denies any summary: a concept is the set of remembered examples, and a new item is assigned to the category whose exemplars it is most similar to overall; Allen and Brooks (1991) showed that similarity to a remembered item can override an explicitly taught rule. Finally, the knowledge / essentialist approach treats concepts as theories: we learn concepts that fit our knowledge more easily (Murphy & Allopenna, 1994) and believe natural kinds have hidden essences (Keil, 1989; Rips, 1989). Most researchers now conclude that concepts are represented through multiple systems — general descriptions, exemplar memories, rules and knowledge — possibly supported by different neural structures (Maddox & Ashby, 2004), and people use prototypes or exemplars depending on circumstances (Malt, 1989).

🖼️ The answer in one picture — and the key points

  • 📏Classical: a definition — necessary and jointly sufficient features; all-or-none.
  • ⭐Prototype: a summary of features weighted by frequency; classify by match. Posner et al. (1967): never-seen prototypes are “recognised”.
  • 🗂️Exemplar: stored examples; summed similarity decides. Allen & Brooks (1991): similarity to an old item overrides a rule.
  • 🧬Knowledge / essentialist: concepts as theories; beliefs about essences can override similarity (Keil, 1989; Rips, 1989).
  • 🤝Consensus: multiple systems — summaries, exemplars and rules together.
Categories are represented not by definitions but by similarity-based summaries (prototypes) and/or remembered exemplars, shaped by world knowledge — most likely through multiple systems.
🪝 Memory hook: rulebook · composite sketch · photo album · detective’s theory.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: “Maybe you store a strict definition, maybe an average bird, maybe every bird you’ve ever seen, or maybe a theory about what makes something a bird. The evidence says the definition idea fails, and we probably use a mix of the others.”
⚠️ Where marks are lost
  • Describing prototype and exemplar views without the difference that matters: summary vs no summary.
  • Leaving out the multiple-systems conclusion.
Objective A3

What are the (dis)advantages of the different views?

Every view wins some findings and loses others — learn which study hurts which theory.

🧭 What the question asks: for each view, what does it explain well and what does it fail to explain — backed by named evidence.

✍️ Answer

Classical view. Advantages: it is simple and precise, works for formal concepts (triangle) and matches people’s intuition that membership is all-or-none. Disadvantages: most categories have no necessary and sufficient features (game, dog, planet); it cannot explain typicality (Rosch, 1973) or fuzzy boundaries and inconsistent judgments (Hampton, 1979; McCloskey & Glucksberg, 1978). Prototype view. Advantages: no definitions are needed; typicality and fuzzy boundaries follow from graded similarity (bowling lies between “sport” and “game”); prototypes are abstracted from experience (Posner et al., 1967) and can be learned from general statements. Disadvantages: an average loses correlations between features (small birds sing, large birds squawk; Mervis & Rosch, 1981), cannot explain context effects (“campfire instrument” → guitar; Roth & Shoben, 1983) or conceptual combination (“pet fish”; Hampton, 1993); it works better as a description than as an explanation. Exemplar view. Advantages: nothing is averaged away, so correlations (Malt & Smith, 1984) and context effects are explained; similarity to stored items overrides rules (Allen & Brooks, 1991); it wins most lab comparisons. Disadvantages: storing every encounter is uneconomical (Komatsu, 1992); it cannot explain recognition of a never-seen prototype; lab studies use few items seen many times (Smith & Minda, 1998). Knowledge / essentialist view. Advantages: explains faster learning of coherent concepts (Murphy & Allopenna, 1994) and why categorisation can override similarity (Keil, 1989; Rips, 1989). Disadvantages: it is less specific about how knowledge is represented, and most categories have no real essence. Overall, both prototypes and exemplars have merit (Malt, 1989), supporting a multiple-systems view.

🖼️ The answer in one picture — and the key points

  • 📏Classical: + precise, fits formal concepts (triangle) and our intuition of all-or-none membership; − no definitions for most categories, no typicality, no fuzzy boundaries.
  • ⭐Prototype: + typicality, fuzzy edges, Posner, learning from statements; − loses feature correlations, ignores context, fails for “pet fish”.
  • 🗂️Exemplar: + correlations, context, Allen & Brooks, wins most lab comparisons; − economy, never-seen prototype, lab tasks with few items.
  • 🧬Knowledge: + coherence effects (donker), essentialism (Keil, Rips); − vague about the actual representation; essences mostly don’t exist.
  • 🤝Conclusion: each has merit — the boundary between theories “is definitely fuzzy” (Robinson-Riegler).
Each view trades off economy against detail: the prototype is compact but loses information; exemplars keep information but are costly — so evidence points to multiple systems.
🪝 Memory hook: prototypes lose the details (correlations, context); exemplars keep too many details (economy).
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: “Definitions are neat but reality isn’t. Averages explain why robins feel birdier than penguins, but they forget that small birds are the singers. Remembering every example fixes that, but it’s a lot of storage. So we probably mix strategies.”
⚠️ Where marks are lost
  • Pros and cons with no study attached.
  • Forgetting the classical view’s advantages (formal concepts, all-or-none intuition).
Objective A4

How do the different views explain the typicality effect?

Typical members are verified faster, learned earlier and named first — who can explain why?

🧭 What the question asks: say, for each view, why typical members are processed faster and judged better — and which view can’t explain it.

✍️ Answer

The typicality effect refers to the finding that typical members (robin) are judged better examples, categorised faster, learned earlier and named first compared with atypical members (penguin) (Rosch, 1973; Murphy’s Text Box 1). The best account of its source is family resemblance: typical items have many features common in their category and few features common in other categories (Rosch & Mervis, 1975); frequency plays only a minor role (Rosch, Simpson & Miller, 1976). The classical view cannot explain typicality, because all members satisfy the same definition and should be equally good. The prototype view explains it directly: typical items share more, and more highly weighted, features with the category prototype, so they match it better and faster; atypical items have fewer such features and may share features with other categories. The exemplar view explains it through summed similarity: typical items resemble many stored exemplars (most birds we have encountered are robin-like), so they are retrieved more readily; it also explains why typicality depends on context (e.g., “campfire musical instrument” → guitar). In semantic network models, typical members are connected to the category node by shorter, stronger links, so activation reaches threshold sooner.

🖼️ The answer in one picture — and the key points

  • 📏Classical: cannot explain it — every member meets the same definition, so all should be equal.
  • ⭐Prototype: typical items share more high-weight features with the prototype → better, faster match. Typicality is “the very basis” of the view.
  • 🗂️Exemplar: typical items resemble many stored exemplars (most birds you know are robin-like) → higher summed similarity; also explains context-dependent typicality.
  • 🕸️Network: typical members have short, strong links to the category node (Lecture 7).
  • 🧬Source: family resemblance, not frequency.
Only the classical view fails; every similarity-based view explains typicality as greater feature overlap between typical members and their category.
🪝 Memory hook: “How close to the ideal?” (prototype) · “How many look-alikes?” (exemplar) · “How short the path?” (network) · “Everyone passes the same test” (classical ✗).
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: “A robin feels more like a bird because it looks like the average bird, looks like most birds you’ve met, and is tightly connected to ‘bird’ in your head. The old definition theory can’t explain that, because to it a bird is a bird.”
⚠️ Where marks are lost
  • Not defining the typicality effect before explaining it.
  • Forgetting to say that the classical view fails — and why.
Part B · Robinson-Riegler, pp. 198–207

B. Networks and concepts

Objective B1

How is semantic knowledge organised in memory?

Robinson-Riegler’s answer: as an associative network of concepts — plus categories of different kinds.

🧭 What the question asks: Robinson-Riegler zooms out from single categories to all knowledge — what is the architecture, and how do we know?

✍️ Answer

Robinson-Riegler describes semantic knowledge as organised in semantic networks: concepts are represented by nodes, and the links between them represent a wide variety of relationships — category membership (canary → bird), properties (yellow → canary) and looser associations (canary → pet → cat). Related concepts are figuratively “close” to each other. In the spreading activation model (Collins & Loftus, 1975) the network is flexible rather than strictly hierarchical, and link length reflects associative strength. The organisation is studied with category verification (“A robin is a bird”) and feature verification tasks (“A cat has pointy ears”), where response speed reveals how closely knowledge is connected: robin-is-bird is verified faster than penguin-is-bird, and pointy ears faster than skin. Semantic networks differ from neural networks (a node is a specific concept rather than a neuron-like unit), but both explain retrieval through excitatory connections and spreading activation. Robinson-Riegler adds that categories are themselves of different types — natural kinds, artifacts, ad hoc categories (Barsalou, 1983) and metaphorical concepts — which differ in how similar their members are (Goldstone & Kersten, 2003). background The earlier hierarchical model of Collins and Quillian (1969) stored properties at the highest level (cognitive economy) but could not explain typicality.

🖼️ The answer in one picture — and the key points

  • 🕸️Semantic network: knowledge stored as an associative network — nodes for concepts, links for relations (canary → bird; yellow → canary; canary → pet → cat).
  • 📏Related concepts are “close” (figuratively); link length reflects associative strength (Collins & Loftus, 1975).
  • ⏱️Tested with category and feature verification; reaction time is the measure.
  • 🧠Not a neural network: a node = one specific concept; but both rely on excitatory connections and spreading activation.
  • 🗂️Categories themselves differ in coherence: natural kinds > artifacts > ad hoc > metaphorical.
Semantic knowledge is organised as an associative network of concept nodes whose link strengths reflect how closely concepts are related — revealed by verification speed.
🪝 Memory hook: a metro map: stations = concepts, lines = relations, and closely linked stations are one stop apart.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: “Picture your knowledge as dots joined by lines — ‘canary’ to ‘bird’, ‘yellow’, ‘pet’. We can tell how close two dots are by timing how fast people say ‘true’ to ‘a canary is a bird’.”
⚠️ Where marks are lost
  • Confusing semantic networks with neural networks.
  • Not mentioning verification tasks as the evidence, or that speed (not accuracy) is measured.
Objective B2

What is the role of similarity in knowledge retrieval?

Similarity decides what comes to mind and what we call things — until a belief about essence overrules it.

🧭 What the question asks: how does similarity decide what we retrieve and how we classify — and where does similarity stop being enough?

✍️ Answer

Similarity plays a role at two levels. First, in the semantic network, similar or related concepts are represented close together and strongly linked, so activating one concept spreads activation to similar ones; this is why typical members (similar to their category) are verified faster and why related words are primed. Second, in categorisation, similarity-based approaches claim that we classify an item by judging its similarity to some standard in long-term memory. The three approaches differ only in the standard: a clearly specified set of defining features (classical view; Bruner et al., 1956), an abstracted best example (prototype view), or the stored members of the category (exemplar view), where the exemplar retrieved depends on context (an eagle → a large bird of prey; Ross & Spalding, 1994). However, similarity is not the whole story. Similarity-based approaches are bottom-up; the essentialist approach argues that categorisation can be top-down, based on beliefs about a concept’s underlying essence (Medin & Ortony, 1989). Rips (1989) demonstrated this with stories of a bird-like creature that came to look like an insect: participants judged it more similar to an insect but still a bird. Similarity is therefore central to retrieval and classification, but can be overridden by deeper knowledge.

🖼️ The answer in one picture — and the key points

  • 🧲In the network: similar/related concepts are close, so retrieving one pre-activates the others — faster verification and priming.
  • 📋In categorisation: similarity-based approaches compare the target with a standard in long-term memory.
  • ⚖️The standard differs: a definition (classical), an abstracted best example (prototype) or all members (exemplar).
  • 🦅Exemplar retrieval depends on context: “is an eagle a bird?” retrieves a large bird of prey (Ross & Spalding, 1994).
  • 🧬Limit: essentialist approaches are top-down — beliefs about the essence can override similarity (Rips, 1989; Medin & Ortony, 1989).
Similarity drives both retrieval (close concepts activate each other) and categorisation (matching a stored standard), but it is not sufficient: beliefs about essences can override it.
🪝 Memory hook: similarity is the magnet; essentialism is the ID card that can overrule it.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: “Mostly we decide what something is by how much it resembles what we know. But if a bird were chemically changed to look like an insect, you’d still call it a bird — so deeper beliefs can beat looks.”
⚠️ Where marks are lost
  • Discussing only categorisation and forgetting retrieval in the network (or vice versa).
  • Not giving the limit of similarity (Rips / essentialism).
Objective B3

How do network models explain typicality and semantic priming?

One mechanism, two effects: activation that spreads, fades, adds up and fires at a threshold.

🧭 What the question asks: use the network’s processing assumptions to explain two effects — faster verification of typical members, and faster recognition of words after a related prime.

✍️ Answer

In the spreading activation model (Collins & Loftus, 1975), concepts are nodes in an associative network, and link length reflects the strength of association. When a concept is presented, its node is activated and activation spreads along the links to related nodes; it weakens with time, distance and the number of concepts activated; activation arriving at a node is summed, and when it exceeds a threshold, that concept is activated. Semantic priming — the finding that processing one stimulus speeds processing of a related one — follows directly: the prime “yellow” activates its node, and activation spreads to linked concepts such as SUN, CANARY and LEMON, giving them a head start toward threshold. When “lemon” is then presented, less additional activation is needed, so it is recognised faster than an unrelated word. The typicality effect follows because typical members are closely linked to their category label: the distance between a category’s prototype and its label is short, so in “A robin is a bird” activation reaches threshold sooner than in “A penguin is a bird”. For the same reason, prototypes are more readily primed by the category name. background A strictly hierarchical network (Collins & Quillian, 1969) would place both robin and penguin one link from BIRD and therefore could not explain typicality — the flexible, strength-based network can.

🖼️ The answer in one picture — and the key points

  • 🟢Priming: the prime (“yellow”) activates its node; activation spreads to SUN, CANARY, LEMON, giving them a head start toward threshold.
  • 🍋When “lemon” appears, less extra activation is needed → recognised faster (“primed and ready”). Unrelated words get no head start.
  • 🐦Typicality: the distance between prototypes and category labels is short → typical members reach threshold first.
  • 🔁Prototypes are also more likely to be primed by the category name.
  • 🌳background A strict hierarchy (Collins & Quillian) places all members one link from the category, so it cannot explain typicality.
Both effects follow from spreading activation to a threshold: priming gives related nodes a head start, and typical members are reached faster because they are more closely linked to their category.
🪝 Memory hook: priming is pre-heating the oven; typicality is living next door.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: “Hearing ‘yellow’ makes ‘lemon’ glow a little in your head, so it lights up faster when it comes. And ‘robin’ sits right next to ‘bird’, so ‘a robin is a bird’ clicks faster than ‘a penguin is a bird’.”
⚠️ Where marks are lost
  • Not stating the processing assumptions (spread, decay, summation, threshold).
  • Explaining priming but forgetting typicality, or vice versa.
Part C · Bozeat et al. (2003), Cognitive Neuropsychology

C. Semantic dementia

Objective C1

What is semantic dementia?

Meaning fades across every modality while perception, reasoning and fluent speech hold up.

🧭 What the question asks: define the disorder — cause, symptoms, what is spared and how it progresses.

✍️ Answer

Semantic dementia (SD) is a progressive neurodegenerative syndrome characterised by a very selective impairment of conceptual knowledge. It is associated with circumscribed atrophy of the polar and inferolateral regions of the temporal lobes, often bilateral and usually more severe on the left. Patients present with a progressive loss of vocabulary affecting both expressive and receptive language, in the context of fluent speech; they show reduced category fluency, anomia and impaired comprehension (e.g., word–picture matching, Pyramids & Palm Trees). The impairment is not limited to words: it appears across modalities — pictures, sounds, object use — with strong item consistency across tasks, which suggests degradation of central conceptual knowledge independent of modality (Bozeat et al., 2000). By contrast, other abilities are relatively spared: visual perception (VOSP, object decision), visuospatial and constructional skills (copying the Rey figure), non-verbal reasoning (Raven’s matrices), working memory and, until late, non-verbal episodic memory. The disorder progresses from specific to general knowledge, as when a patient names a duck “duck”, then “swan”, then “bird” and finally “animal” over 18 months (Hodges et al., 1995). This selectivity makes SD a particularly informative testing ground for the structure of semantic memory.

🖼️ The answer in one picture — and the key points

  • 🧩Definition: a progressive neurodegenerative syndrome with a very selective impairment of conceptual knowledge.
  • 🧠Cause: circumscribed atrophy of the polar and inferolateral temporal lobes, usually left > right.
  • 🗣️Presentation: progressive loss of vocabulary (expressive and receptive) with fluent speech.
  • 🌐Amodal: deficits across words, pictures, sounds and object use, with the same items failing across tasks (Bozeat et al., 2000).
  • 📉Course: specific before general — duck → swan → bird → animal.
Semantic dementia is a progressive, selective loss of conceptual knowledge across modalities, caused by anterior temporal atrophy, with speech fluency, perception and reasoning relatively spared.
🪝 Memory hook: a photo slowly going out of focus: fine details first, then only the outline remains.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: “It’s a dementia that eats away at what words and things mean because part of the temporal lobe shrinks. People still talk fluently and can copy drawings, but slowly forget what a duck or a kettle is.”
⚠️ Where marks are lost
  • Describing SD as “memory loss”: everyday and episodic memory are relatively spared.
  • Forgetting the neural basis or the cross-modal nature.
Objective C2

What can we learn from semantic dementia about semantic memory?

The way knowledge falls apart shows how it was built.

🧭 What the question asks: which conclusions about the healthy semantic system follow from the pattern of loss in SD — each with its evidence?

✍️ Answer

Bozeat et al. (2003) asked SD patients to draw objects and scored the drawings by features. Several lessons follow. (1) Semantic memory is a unitary, amodal system: drawing correlated with naming and word–picture matching at r = .96–.99, and error patterns in drawing mirrored those in naming, definitions and sorting, so all tasks draw on one central store rather than separate verbal and visual systems. (2) It is dissociable from other cognition: immediate copying, delayed copying of meaningless geometric shapes, copying the Rey figure and Raven’s matrices were relatively preserved, whereas performance declined as tasks relied more on meaning (immediate copy > delayed copy > drawing to name). (3) Concepts are distributed patterns of features: errors were omissions and intrusions of individual features, and categories and typicality can emerge from feature overlap without explicit category labels (Garrard et al., 2001; Rogers et al., 2002a). (4) Shared features are robust and distinctive features fragile: distinctive features were omitted most and domain-shared features least, explaining the specific-to-general loss (duck → bird → animal) and box-like artefacts. (5) Degradation pulls concepts toward the prototype: domain-shared features intruded (four legs on a duck), paralleling the over-extension of typical names. (6) Apparent category-specific effects can arise from feature distribution: animals showed more intrusions, but this disappeared once distinctiveness was controlled, because animals have more shared features (0.59 vs 0.29 per item). The authors cannot rule out a separate structural description system (Riddoch et al., 1988), but prefer an interactive account in which visual representations depend on semantic feedback.

🖼️ The answer in one picture — and the key points

  • 🌐Unitary and amodal: drawing, naming and matching decline together (r = .96–.99); error patterns match across verbal and non-verbal tasks.
  • 🧱Dissociable: immediate copying, meaningless-shape recall, Rey copy and Raven’s are spared.
  • ☁️Distributed features: concepts are overlapping features; categories and typicality emerge from overlap (Garrard et al., 2001; Rogers et al., 2002a).
  • 🎯Shared robust, distinctive fragile: distinctive features omitted most; domain-shared features intrude → drift toward the prototype.
  • 🐾Domain effects come from feature distribution: the animal > artefact intrusion effect vanishes when distinctiveness is controlled.
Semantic dementia shows that meaning is stored in one amodal but distributed network of features, in which shared features are robust and distinctive features fragile — the same overlap that produces typicality.
🪝 Memory hook: erosion on a sandcastle: the flags and towers (distinctive features) go first; the broad base (shared features) goes last.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: “The disease doesn’t delete random facts. It first removes what makes a duck different from other birds, then what makes it a bird, and the leftover ‘generic animal’ bits even get added to the wrong things. So concepts must be built from overlapping features, and shared ones are the sturdiest.”
⚠️ Where marks are lost
  • Giving lessons without evidence from the study (numbers, tasks).
  • Forgetting the geometric-shape control that rules out an episodic explanation.
  • Not mentioning the rival account.
Putting A, B and C together

🧵 Synthesis

Thesis: semantic memory is a network of concepts built from overlapping features, and feature overlap — similarity — explains how categories are structured, represented, retrieved and lost. Murphy shows that categories cannot be defined: they have fuzzy boundaries and a graded internal structure, because members differ in how many common features they share (family resemblance). They sit in hierarchies with a knowledge-dependent basic level and within our wider theories of the world. Prototype and exemplar views are two ways of storing that overlap — a summary versus remembered examples — each with typical strengths and weaknesses; essentialism adds that beliefs about hidden causes can override surface similarity. Robinson-Riegler turns the same principle into an architecture. Similar concepts lie close together in a network, so activation spreads to related nodes: typical members reach their category fastest, and a prime gives related words a head start. Bozeat et al. provide the most striking confirmation. When semantic dementia degrades this single, amodal store, the distinctive features that separate a duck from a swan disappear first, while the shared, prototypical core survives and even intrudes — a duck with four legs. Typicality, the basic level, priming and the pattern of semantic loss are four faces of one principle: meaning is built from shared features, and what is shared is what is strongest.
